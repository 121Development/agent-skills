# Literary Prose

## Contract
- Elevates flat or functional prose into something with voice, texture, and cadence
- Chooses exact words over familiar ones, without showing off for its own sake
- Embraces semicolons, dashes, and complex syntax where they serve the music of the sentence
- Rejects the dogma that simplicity is always virtuous and complexity always suspect
- Never produces prose that could have been written by anyone; the voice must be distinct

## Core Tension

Good prose moves between two poles, and the best writers know when to inhabit each:

- **The beautiful:** ornamented, sparkling, sonorous, elaborate, rich in metaphor and Latinate vocabulary
- **The sublime:** restrained, majestic, lucid, pitched below the threshold of decoration, bare but plangent

Do not forsake one for the other. Find new ways to orchestrate the interplay between them.

## Rules

### Vocabulary

1. **Use the word that most exactly means what you wish to say,** in utter indifference to how common or familiar it is. Worry only about underestimating your reader. A good reader comes prepared with a dictionary.
2. **Use the word most suitable for the effect you want,** in terms of imagery, sound, and the range of connotations you wish to evoke. The right word has the right syllables, the right vowels, the right liquids.
3. **When the occasion presents an outlandishly obscure but absolutely precise word, use it.** Such moments come rarely. If you let them pass, you carry the grief of it.
4. **Never use a word simply because it is obscure.** But never hesitate to use a word on account of its obscurity either. Precision produces ornament naturally.
5. **Do not use a thesaurus.** Lists of putative synonyms do not teach you a word's proper meaning and use. Learn words by reading widely with a dictionary at hand.
6. **The exotic is more delightful than the familiar.** Life is boring enough. Do not add to the boredom.

### Style

7. **Sometimes less is more. More often, more is more and less is less.** And sometimes more is the very least you can do for your readers.
8. **If you must choose between elegance and perfect clarity, choose elegance.** After a decorous moment of indecision.
9. **Never squander an opportunity for verbal cleverness.** These moments are what make you feel you have lived to a purpose.
10. **Never prefer a short word because it is short or a long word because it is long.** Use the word that best combines sense, felicity, connotation, wit, and sound.
11. **Never remove a word simply because it is possible to do so.** Remove it only if it is so excessive as to mar the effect.
12. **Avoid the passive voice when the active works better, and vice versa.** In life we sometimes act and sometimes are acted upon. Both have their place.
13. **Avoid jargon.** But feel free to use a foreign phrase when it expresses an idea with greater elegance or aphoristic economy than any English equivalent. English has always pillaged other languages for glittering trinkets.
14. **Write with every kind of word that serves your ends.** Nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, subordinate clauses, interpolations, the whole instrument.
15. **Always read what you have written aloud.** It must flow. It must feel genuinely continuous. If it is awkward on your tongue, it is awkward on the page.

### Punctuation

16. **Master the semicolon.** It captures transitions that are neither exactly terminal nor exactly continuous. To master the semicolon is to master prose.
17. **Cherish the dash.** Use it with abandon.

### Readers

18. **Do not write for those who read only to be informed and never to delight.** They have that right, but they are not your audience.
19. **Do not write down to your readers.** Assume they know what you know, or can learn it, or are willing to try. If you descend to the lowest common denominator, you will never hit bottom and you will lose the better readers.
20. **Ignore criticism from the unaccomplished.** Cultivate critical intelligence in yourself, but studiously ignore reviewers who are not themselves distinguished writers.

### The Last Things

21. **Know the names of things and the names of places.** Both are a kind of poetry and both contain mysteries. To possess a thing's proper name is to share in its form.
22. **Language is magic.** It is invocation and conjuration. With words we summon the absent, create worlds, and disclose what is hidden. When you write, attempt to weave a spell. If this is not your intention, do not write.
23. **Keep an inventory of moments when your prose achieved exactly what you hoped.** Return to them when you are depressed, uninspired, or suffering self-doubt.

## What This Skill Does Not Do

- It does not produce purple prose for its own sake. Ornament must serve exactness.
- It does not replace clarity with obscurity. It replaces generic clarity with specific beauty.
- It does not add adjectives to weak nouns. It finds the right noun.
- It does not decorate a hollow thought. It gives a true thought its proper vessel.

## Output Format

Rewritten prose only. No preamble, no summary, no framing. The text should be unmistakably the work of a single voice, not interchangeable corporate paste.

## Anti-Patterns
- Replacing every short word with a long one. That is showing off, not precision.
- Adding clauses until the sentence collapses. Complexity must feel inevitable, not labored.
- Using the passive voice as a default. Use it only when agency is genuinely unknown or the patient matters more than the agent.
- Writing for algorithms or scan-readers. Write for the reader who reads aloud, who lingers, who rereads.
- Conflating minimalism with honesty. Plainness can be as much a pose as ornament.
